Pedagogy Judges: A Multi-Model LLM Ensemble for Scoring Teaching Quality

Aryan Kumar

Research Question

Large language models are increasingly used to evaluate teaching quality in AI-tutoring transcripts, but a single LLM's judgment is an unreliable ground truth — it reflects one model's biases and blind spots, not a validated measure. This project asks: can independent scores from multiple, architecturally different LLMs be combined to produce a more trustworthy pedagogy assessment, and where do such models agree or disagree?

Methodology

A 6-factor pedagogy rubric (1–5 scale per factor) was built on established teaching-quality research: PCK (Shulman), Feedback (Hattie's Visible Learning), Modeling and Coaching (Cognitive Apprenticeship), Reflection (student articulation of reasoning), and Engagement (the ICAP framework's Passive→Active→Constructive→Interactive scale). The overall Pedagogy Score is (sum of 6 factor scores / 30) × 100.

Three judges — OpenAI (gpt-5-mini), Groq (gpt-oss-120b), and Google Gemini — each receive the identical system prompt and rubric and independently score the same lesson transcript, returning structured JSON with a score, confidence, evidence, reasoning, strengths, weaknesses, and suggestions per factor. Because all three see the same instructions, any disagreement between them reflects genuine differences in model judgment rather than prompt variation. Per-factor scores are aggregated by mean, with the standard deviation across the three judges reported as an inter-rater agreement signal.

The three judges reached exact unanimous agreement on half the rubric (PCK, Feedback, Coaching). Disagreement was mild and consistent everywhere it occurred: on Modeling, Reflection, and Engagement, two judges agreed and one differed by exactly one point — no judge diverged by more than a single point on any factor. This is the intended signal of the multi-judge design: it surfaces exactly where an evaluation is contestable instead of presenting a single confident-looking number.

Limitations & Next Steps

  • No human ground truth yet. Judges agreeing with each other shows consistency, not correctness — the next step is comparing judge scores to real human expert ratings.
  • Aggregation is currently a plain mean. Each judge already outputs a confidence score per factor that the aggregation step does not yet use.
  • Single-transcript demonstration. A larger paired teacher-student dataset (278 experiment runs across multiple student/tutor model combinations) is already collected and ready for batch evaluation.
  • Per-episode scoring (scoring one question within a multi-question session) is not yet implemented.
